\begin{abstract}
How good does a large language model (LLM) have to be before it is worth putting inside a
scientific agent? We argue the question is mis-posed: what matters is not how often the model is
right, but whether the surrounding architecture can throw its errors away for free. We test this
in active causal discovery, where an agent spends a small budget of interventions to identify an
unknown causal graph. \nem{} confines the LLM to a single job --- proposing which adjacencies a
statistical estimator probably got wrong --- and hands every numerical decision to exact
machinery: a Bayesian score ranks the resulting candidate graphs, expected information gain picks
each experiment, and a closed-form likelihood updates the belief. Auditing the proposals against
ground truth, we find they are barely better than guessing: across $960$ episodes
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} proposes $236$ edits of which $11$ are correct ($4.7\%$, $1.23\times$ the
rate of an uninformed editor). Yet the same system reaches $0.950$ directed-edge F1 against
$0.857$ for the strongest classical active baseline ($+0.093$, $p{=}0.0008$, paired) and $0.000$
--- $0.180$ for the same models run end-to-end, using $32\times$ fewer tokens and one LLM call per
episode. Controls explain the paradox. Random edits at the same rate buy nothing ($+0.021$, n.s.),
so the content of the proposals does matter; a guard that reserves half the hypothesis budget for
the unedited estimate bounds what a bad proposal can cost; and the true graph enters the candidate
set $15$ points more often, because one right guess out of $236$ is enough. The value is also
bounded and predictable: a perfect proposer would gain $+0.109$, and the LLM's contribution decays
to zero as observational data grows, tracking the point where its edits stop beating chance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An autonomous scientific agent has to do three separable things: say what might be true, decide
which experiment would best discriminate between the possibilities, and update its beliefs from
what comes back. Language models are now inserted into all three roles, and are usually evaluated
by a single end-to-end score. That score answers the wrong question. It tells us whether a
particular assembly worked, not which role the model was suited to --- and the roles have very
different tolerances for error.

The difference is one of \emph{error cost}. When a model \emph{decides} something, a wrong output
propagates directly into the answer: there is nothing downstream to catch it. When a model
\emph{proposes} something, a wrong output is one candidate among many, and any downstream filter
that can score candidates against data discards it at no cost. A proposer therefore does not need
to be accurate. It needs to be better than nothing, occasionally, in a way the filter can exploit.
This paper makes that intuition quantitative in a setting where every step can be measured
exactly.

\emph{Active causal discovery} is that setting. Observational data alone cannot tell $A \to B$
from $B \to A$; interventions can. An agent is given a sample of observational data and a budget
of a few interventions, and must return the directed graph. The task is small enough that the
exact Bayesian solution is computable, so an LLM's contribution can be isolated against a fully
mechanical version of the identical pipeline --- the control that end-to-end evaluations lack. We
anonymize the variables as $X_0 \dots X_{d-1}$ so that no answer can be recalled from world
knowledge, and later put real names back to measure what that removed.

Our companion study, \rauma{}, established the negative half of the argument: granting an LLM
authority to \emph{state} the graph from experimental evidence costs $0.32$ F1, because the models
cannot judge whether a measured mean has moved relative to its sampling scale. The natural
follow-up is the positive half. If the model should not decide, and choosing experiments turns out
to be a solved problem, what is left for it to do?

We answer: supply the hypothesis space. \nem{} shows the LLM the correlation and partial-correlation
matrices of the observational sample together with the adjacency set the PC algorithm estimated
from it, and asks one question --- which of those adjacencies are wrong? Every candidate graph the
edits generate is then scored by an exact linear-Gaussian Bayesian criterion, each experiment is
chosen to maximize expected information gain about which candidate is true, and each outcome
updates the posterior in closed form. The model never sees interventional data and never writes an
edge into the answer.

The result is a system whose proposer is almost useless in isolation and whose overall accuracy is
nonetheless state of the art for this benchmark. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{A near-chance proposer that still wins.} Audited against ground truth, LLM skeleton
edits are correct $4.7\%$--$6.3\%$ of the time, only $1.19$--$1.23\times$ the rate of an
uninformed editor. The full system nonetheless beats the strongest classical active baseline by
$0.093$ F1 ($p{=}0.0008$) and an end-to-end LLM agent by $0.74$--$0.95$ F1
(Section~\ref{sec:main}, Section~\ref{sec:wrong}).
\item \textbf{Controls that say why.} Random edits at the same rate produce no significant gain,
so width alone is not the mechanism; removing the guard that reserves part of the hypothesis
budget for the unedited estimate makes a bad proposal expensive again; and dropping the Bayesian
update costs $0.34$ F1, the largest single effect in the system
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{A boundary, stated quantitatively.} The LLM's contribution falls from $+0.058$ to
$-0.024$ F1 as the observational sample grows from $40$ to $1000$, tracking the decay of its edit
precision from $1.8\times$ chance to below chance. A perfect proposer would gain $+0.109$ in the
main regime, so the channel is real, bounded, and mostly unexploited
(Section~\ref{sec:crossover}).
\item \textbf{Cost.} One LLM call and $\approx\!1.1$k tokens per episode, against $3.8$ calls and
$35$k tokens for the end-to-end agent (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background, in plain terms}
\label{sec:background}

We keep the main text readable without prior exposure to causal discovery; formal statements are
in Appendix~\ref{app:background}.

\paragraph{What observation can and cannot tell you.}
Suppose $d$ variables are generated by an unknown directed acyclic graph (DAG): each variable is a
noisy linear function of its parents. From observational data one can recover which pairs are
\emph{directly connected} --- the \emph{skeleton} --- and the direction of some, but not all,
edges. The graphs that remain indistinguishable form an \emph{equivalence class}, summarized by a
\emph{CPDAG}: arrows that every member agrees on, plus undirected edges that no amount of further
observation can orient. Orienting those is what experiments are for.

\paragraph{What an intervention buys.}
Forcing $X_a$ to an unusual value and re-sampling makes the true children of $a$ shift their
distribution while non-children do not. One such experiment therefore settles the direction of the
edges adjacent to $a$, and the acyclicity of the graph often settles further edges for free.

\paragraph{Two things the agent must supply.}
A \emph{candidate set} $\Hyp$ of graphs it considers possible, and a \emph{policy} for spending
its interventions. The ceiling of any such agent is set by $\Hyp$: if the true graph is not in it,
no experiment can recover it. This is why the hypothesis space --- not the experiment policy --- is
where we place the model.

\paragraph{The statistical front-end, and why it is fallible.}
We estimate the initial skeleton with PC, a standard constraint-based algorithm that removes an
adjacency when the two variables look conditionally independent. With finite samples its
independence tests are underpowered, so it misses real edges. On our instances PC recovers the
skeleton with F1 $0.938$ and misses $27$ true adjacencies across $40$ instances --- competent, but
wrong often enough that there is something for a proposer to fix.

\paragraph{1. Propose (the only LLM call).}
The model receives the correlation matrix, the partial correlation of every pair given all other
variables, PC's adjacency set, and the list of currently non-adjacent pairs. It returns two lists:
adjacencies to remove and pairs to add, at most four each. It is told the relevant fact --- in a
linear-Gaussian DAG two variables are directly connected exactly when their partial correlation
given the rest is non-zero --- and told that an empty list is a valid answer. It is never shown
interventional data. Prompts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\paragraph{2. Enumerate and score.}
Let $S$ be PC's skeleton. We form $S$ itself, each single edit applied to $S$, and the all-edits
variant, and enumerate acyclic orientations of each (exhaustively when cheap, sampled otherwise).
Every candidate DAG is fitted by least squares and scored by the linear-Gaussian BIC on the same
observational sample, giving a posterior $w_h \propto \exp(-\tfrac12 \mathrm{BIC}_h)$ over a
candidate set $\Hyp$ of at most $48$ graphs.

\emph{The guard.} Half of $|\Hyp|$ is reserved for the best-scoring orientations of the
\emph{unedited} skeleton before any edited variant is admitted. A proposal can therefore only ever
\emph{add} candidates; it can never evict the graphs the statistics already supported. This single
design choice is what converts a wrong proposal from a liability into a wasted slot, and we ablate
it directly in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\paragraph{3. Choose.}
For each variable $a$ still ambiguous under $w$, we estimate the expected information gain of
$\mathrm{do}(X_a = v)$ about the hypothesis index by Monte Carlo over $12$ simulated outcomes, and
intervene on the maximizer. This is the exact Bayesian design criterion for this model class, not
a heuristic.

\paragraph{4. Update and submit.}
The interventional batch is scored under each candidate's mutilated linear-Gaussian likelihood in
closed form and $w$ is multiplied through. The loop stops when the budget is spent or the
posterior concentrates ($\max_h w_h > 0.999$); the MAP graph is submitted.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Instances.}
Four graph sizes, $d \in \{4,6,8,10\}$ with $|E| = d$ edges, ten accepted instances each, giving
$40$ paired instances. Instances are rejected unless they are non-trivial: the equivalence class must
leave at least two undirected edges, those edges must not all meet at one node, the skeleton must
be connected, and at least one intervention must be required. Edge weights are drawn away from
zero so the model is faithful. The default sample is $n_{\mathrm{obs}} = 300$
observational rows and $n_{\mathrm{int}} = 150$ rows per intervention. The intervention budget is
$|I^*| + 1$, where $I^*$ is a minimum identifying intervention set --- on average $2.8$
experiments. Variables are randomly permuted and named $X_0 \dots X_{d-1}$. Details and the
difficulty ladder are in Appendix~\ref{app:instances}.

\paragraph{Arms.}
Every arm runs on every instance, so all comparisons are paired. \emph{Baselines}: an oracle
ceiling; \texttt{PC+greedy}, the classical active baseline that intervenes on the highest-degree
ambiguous node and orients by a mean-shift test (with and without Meek closure); and
\texttt{LLM-e2e}, the same models given the raw data and full control of the loop.
\emph{Hypothesis-space arms}, which share \nem's entire decision layer and differ only in where
candidates come from: \texttt{no-LLM} (orientations of PC's unedited skeleton), \texttt{MEC-only}
(PC's equivalence class), \texttt{LLM-graphs} (the model writes whole DAGs instead of edits),
\texttt{random-DAGs}, and the proposal-content controls \texttt{random-edits} and
\texttt{perfect-edits} described in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. \emph{Decision-layer arms} hold
the candidate set fixed and remove one mechanism at a time.

\paragraph{Models and protocol.}
We use two inexpensive models at temperature zero with forced tool calls: one open-weight
(\texttt{qwen3-coder-30b}) and one proprietary (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}); full provider identifiers
are in Appendix~\ref{app:instances}. Within
an instance every ablation arm replays one shared proposal call, so arms differ only in the
component under test and not in provider non-determinism. Error bars are $95\%$ CIs, over
instances for arm means and over paired differences for contrasts; pairwise claims use two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with win/loss/tie counts, because ties are frequent and a $p$-value
alone hides them. The main study is $960$ episodes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{\nem{} beats both the classical and the end-to-end agent}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:main}(a) give the headline. \nem{} with
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} reaches $0.950$ directed F1 against $0.857$ for \texttt{PC+greedy}
($+0.093$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.046,+0.148]$, $p{=}0.0008$, W/L/T $14/1/25$) and $0.916$ with
\texttt{qwen3-30b} ($+0.059$, $p{=}0.047$). Against the same models run end-to-end on the same
instances the gap is $+0.950$ and $+0.736$: an unscaffolded \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} scores exactly
zero, submitting no correct arrow in any episode.

Two comparisons inside the table matter more than the baselines. Replacing the LLM's edits with
nothing --- the \texttt{no-LLM} arm, which shares every other component --- costs $0.060$ F1
($[+0.023,+0.108]$, $p{=}0.0075$, $9/0/31$). That difference is the entire measurable contribution
of the language model to this system. And asking the same model for whole DAGs instead of edits
costs $0.300$ F1: the interface, not the model, is what makes the proposal usable.

Figure~\ref{fig:main}(b) addresses the natural objection that \nem{} simply spends more of its
budget ($2.55$ interventions against $1.73$). It does not win by spending more: after a
\emph{single} intervention \nem{} is already at $0.856$ F1, matching what \texttt{PC+greedy}
reaches with its entire budget, and after two it is at $0.922$ while the baseline plateaus at
$0.858$. It is ahead at every experiment count, and continues to buy accuracy where the baseline
has stopped.

\subsection{The proposals are barely better than guessing}
\label{sec:wrong}

The result above is easy to over-read as ``the LLM knows something about causal structure''. We
audited every proposal against the ground truth it was never shown, and it does not.

Across the $40$ main instances \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} proposed $236$ edits, of which $11$ were
correct: a precision of $4.7\%$. \texttt{qwen3-30b} proposed $160$ and got $10$ right ($6.3\%$).
Precision alone is not interpretable, because the base rate of being right depends on how many
errors PC actually made, so we compare against an \emph{uninformed editor} that spends its edits
on uniformly random pairs drawn from the same sets. That editor would score $3.8\%$ and $5.2\%$
respectively. The models are therefore operating at $1.23\times$ and $1.19\times$ chance
(Figure~\ref{fig:wrong}(a)).

The asymmetry is instructive. Removals are worthless: of $141$ adjacencies \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}
proposed deleting, $1$ was a genuine PC false positive --- slightly \emph{below} chance, and PC
only made two such errors in total. All of the signal is in additions, where the model recovers
$10$ of PC's $27$ missed adjacencies ($37\%$ recall) while proposing $95$. The model is a
high-recall, very-low-precision detector of missing edges, which is exactly the profile a
generate-and-filter architecture can use and a decide-directly architecture cannot.

That profile pays off at the level of the \emph{set} rather than the edit
(Figure~\ref{fig:wrong}(b)). The true DAG lies in the candidate set for $42.5\%$ of instances
without the LLM and $57.5\%$ with it, a $15$-point gain ($p{=}0.014$) bought by $11$ correct
guesses out of $236$. One right guess per instance is all the downstream machinery needs; the
other $225$ occupy slots and are then scored away.

\subsection{Why being wrong is cheap}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Three mechanisms make a near-chance proposer safe. We isolate each.

\paragraph{Is it the content, or just a wider space?}
The obvious deflationary explanation is that any extra candidates help, and the LLM is only a
random-graph generator with extra steps. We test it directly with \texttt{random-edits}: the same
pipeline, the same number of removals and additions, drawn uniformly at random from the same
pairs. It does not reproduce the effect. In the main regime random edits gain $+0.021$ over no
edits ($[-0.025,+0.072]$, $p{=}0.48$) against the LLM's $+0.060$ ($p{=}0.0075$), and at
$n_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}60$ they \emph{lose} $0.067$ ($p{=}0.051$). Interestingly, random editing does
raise candidate-set containment (from $0.425$ to $0.500$), so it widens the space in the intended
sense --- it just dilutes it at the same time. The LLM's containment gain is twice as large and is
the one that converts into accuracy.

\paragraph{Does the guard matter?}
Half the hypothesis budget is reserved for the unedited skeleton
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). Turning that reservation off lets proposals compete for every slot.
With perfect edits the guard is nearly irrelevant ($0.998$ either way) --- there is nothing to
protect against. With random edits it is worth $0.058$ F1 ($0.910$ with, $0.852$ without), and
without it the true DAG is pushed out of the candidate set outright in a substantial fraction of
instances (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}(a)). The guard is precisely the component that makes the
system's tolerance for bad proposals a design property rather than a lucky accident.

\paragraph{What does the decision layer contribute?}
Table~\ref{tab:decision} holds the candidate set fixed and removes one mechanism at a time. The
Bayesian update dominates everything: without it the system keeps its BIC ranking, performs its
experiments, and ignores the results, scoring $0.609$ --- a loss of $0.341$, larger than the gap
between \nem{} and an untooled LLM agent. Re-scoring candidates by BIC rather than a uniform prior
is worth $0.017$. Choosing experiments by expected information gain beats random selection by
$0.049$, but beats a one-line max-degree heuristic by only $0.005$ (n.s.), reproducing our
companion study's finding that experiment \emph{selection} is not where these systems fail.
Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}(b) shows the corresponding posterior collapse: $2.32$ nats at the
start, $1.01$ after one experiment, $0.31$ after two, $0.14$ after three.

\subsection{Where the proposal channel earns its keep --- and where it stops}
\label{sec:crossover}

An LLM that edits a statistical estimate can only help to the extent the estimate is wrong. We
sweep the observational sample size from $40$ to $1000$ rows on fixed instances, which moves PC's
skeleton quality without touching anything else (Figure~\ref{fig:crossover}).

The contribution decays monotonically and changes sign. At $n_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}40$ the LLM is
worth $+0.052$ (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}) and $+0.058$ (\texttt{qwen3-30b}, $p{=}0.008$) over the
identical pipeline without it. By $n_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}120$ the gain is indistinguishable from
zero, and at $300$ and $1000$ it is negative ($-0.030$ and $-0.024$). Panel (b) supplies the
mechanism: edit precision relative to chance falls from $1.8\times$ at $n{=}40$ to $1.1\times$ at
$n{=}300$ and to at-or-below chance at $n{=}1000$. When PC has few errors left to find, the
model's proposals are indistinguishable from noise, and noise is not free --- it consumes
hypothesis slots.

This also explains why the main table, at $n_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}300$, still shows a significant
$+0.060$: it spans $d{=}4$ to $d{=}10$, and PC's difficulty is set by the ratio of data to graph
size rather than by sample size alone. The general statement is not ``LLMs help when data is
scarce'' but ``LLMs help exactly where the statistical front-end is underpowered''.

Two further readings of the same figure are worth stating plainly. First, the \emph{perfect-edits}
arm gains $+0.231$ at $n{=}40$ and $+0.109$ in the main regime, reaching $0.998$ F1 --- meaning
that once the skeleton is right, this pipeline recovers the DAG essentially always, and
\emph{every remaining error in the system is an adjacency error}. Second, against that ceiling the
LLM captures roughly a quarter of the available gain at $n{=}40$ and just over half in the main
regime. The proposal channel is real, it is worth more than any model we tested extracts from it,
and it is the right place to aim a better proposer.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

\nem{} makes one forced tool call per episode and spends $1{,}114$ tokens
(\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}) or $1{,}843$ (\texttt{qwen3-30b}), about \$0.0002--\$0.0007. The end-to-end
agent spends $3.8$ calls and $35$k--$52$k tokens for a worse answer: $32\times$ and $28\times$
more tokens for $0.95$ and $0.74$ less F1. The reason is structural rather than a matter of prompt
economy --- an end-to-end agent must carry every interventional batch through its context window,
while \nem{} never shows the model interventional data at all. The remaining computation is
milliseconds of exact enumeration and scoring.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Active causal discovery.}
Choosing interventions to identify a graph is a long-standing problem, addressed by
coverage arguments over the equivalence class, by information-theoretic acquisition, by Bayesian
experimental design over structure posteriors, and more recently by learned or amortized
acquisition policies. Our decision layer is deliberately a textbook instance of the Bayesian
branch rather than a contribution: it is the fixed, exact yardstick against which the proposal
channel is measured.

\paragraph{LLMs and causality.}
Language models have been used as semantic causal experts that answer pairwise
cause-effect questions, as priors or initializers for structure learning, as search operators
over graphs, and as end-to-end agents driving a discovery loop. Evaluations typically report a
final graph or task score on named variables, where the answer is partly recoverable from world
knowledge. We anonymize variables to close that channel, and then reopen it deliberately as a
measured condition rather than an uncontrolled confound.

\paragraph{Propose-and-verify architectures.}
The pattern of generating many candidates cheaply and filtering them with a trusted verifier
recurs across program synthesis, theorem proving and reasoning-with-verifiers. What that
literature mostly measures is end-to-end pass rate. The quantity we think matters ---
\emph{how accurate does the generator have to be, given the verifier} --- is rarely measured
directly, because in most of those domains the ground truth of an individual proposal is not
observable. Causal discovery on synthetic instances is unusual in that it is, which is what lets
us report a proposer's precision and its downstream value in the same experiment.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The number we find most useful is not $0.950$ but $4.7\%$. A component that is right one time in
twenty is, by any ordinary standard, not working --- and it lifts a well-tuned classical pipeline
by $0.093$ F1 while costing a thousand tokens. What makes that possible is not the model. It is
that the architecture puts the model somewhere its errors are cheap: a wrong proposal occupies a
slot in a candidate set and is then scored away by a criterion the model does not control, using
data the model never sees.

Read alongside our companion study, the pair gives a fairly sharp allocation rule. Giving an LLM
authority over the \emph{readout} --- letting it state conclusions from experimental evidence ---
costs $0.32$ F1, because a wrong output there goes straight into the answer. Giving it authority
over \emph{proposals} gains $0.06$--$0.09$ F1 at $4.7\%$ accuracy, because a wrong output there is
free. Neither number is about how smart the model is. Both are about how much a mistake costs
where it is made. We would suggest that ``where can this component be wrong cheaply?'' is a more
productive design question for LLM scientific agents than ``is this component accurate enough?''.

The limits are equally clear, and we would rather state them than have them found. The LLM's
contribution is not significant for every model we tested, it decays to zero and then to negative
as the statistical front-end strengthens, and a perfect proposer would be worth roughly twice what
the best of ours delivered. The exact posterior that makes the filtering possible is affordable
only for small graphs; scaling this design past $d \approx 12$ requires replacing exact
enumeration with a sampler, which weakens the very guarantee the argument rests on. And our
proposer is being asked to do statistics rather than recall domain facts, which is plausibly the
task LLMs are worst at --- the version of this system that matters scientifically is one where
the variables mean something.

\section{Formal background}
\label{app:background}

\paragraph{Model class.}
A linear-Gaussian structural causal model over a DAG $G$ on $d$ nodes sets
$X_j = \sum_{i \in \mathrm{pa}_G(j)} \beta_{ij} X_i + \varepsilon_j$ with
$\varepsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_j^2)$ independent. We assume causal sufficiency (no
hidden confounders), faithfulness (no conditional independence beyond those implied by $G$), and
full observability.

\paragraph{Equivalence.}
Two DAGs are Markov equivalent iff they share a skeleton and a set of unshielded colliders
($i \to k \leftarrow j$ with $i,j$ non-adjacent). The equivalence class is represented by a CPDAG:
edges compelled in every member are directed, the rest undirected. Observational data identify $G$
only up to its CPDAG.

\paragraph{Interventions.}
A hard intervention $\mathrm{do}(X_a = v)$ replaces $a$'s structural equation by the constant $v$,
mutilating $G$ by deleting edges into $a$. Under our assumptions the interventional distribution
of an adjacent $X_b$ shifts iff $a \to b$, which is what makes an experiment informative about
orientation.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
For a candidate $h$ we fit $\hat\beta, \hat\sigma^2$ by least squares and use
$\mathrm{BIC}_h = -2 \ell_h + k_h \log n$ with $k_h$ the parameter count, treating
$w_h \propto \exp(-\tfrac12 \mathrm{BIC}_h)$ as a posterior over $\Hyp$ under a uniform structure
prior. This is the standard BIC approximation to the marginal likelihood, exact up to $O(1)$ for
this model class.

\paragraph{Expected information gain.}
Writing $H$ for the index of the true hypothesis and $Y_a$ for the batch returned by
$\mathrm{do}(X_a = v)$, we choose
$a_t = \arg\max_a\; \mathbb{H}[H] - \mathbb{E}_{Y_a}\,\mathbb{H}[H \mid Y_a]$,
estimating the expectation by sampling $12$ outcomes from the current posterior predictive. Both
entropies are over the finite candidate set, so this is exact up to Monte Carlo error.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\emph{Directed-edge F1} scores submitted arrows against $G$: an arrow is correct only if both its
adjacency and its orientation are right. \emph{Skeleton F1} scores adjacencies only, ignoring
direction, and therefore upper-bounds directed F1. \emph{SHD} counts the single-edge operations
needed to turn the submission into $G$.

\section{Instance generation and the difficulty ladder}
\label{app:instances}

Instances are sampled and then filtered by a fixed acceptance policy, identical for every arm.
A candidate is rejected if its CPDAG leaves fewer than two undirected edges (too little for
experiments to do), if every undirected edge meets the same node (one intervention would settle
everything), if its skeleton is disconnected, if no intervention is needed at all, or if any
relevant structural partial correlation falls below a faithfulness margin of $0.1$. Edge weights are drawn from
$(-2,-0.5) \cup (0.5,2)$ and noise variances are unit.

The two proposers are \texttt{qwen/qwen3-coder-30b-a3b-instruct} and
\texttt{openai/gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18}, both served through OpenRouter.

Density is $|E| = d$ by design. This calibrates PC to be a competent but imperfect front-end: it
achieves skeleton F1 $0.938$ at $n_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}300$, leaves $3$--$5$ undirected edges,
and the true DAG lies in the equivalence class PC estimates in only $40\%$ of instances. At higher density
($|E| \approx 1.4d$) PC's skeleton is wrong so often that every arm is capped at the same low
ceiling and no experiment can help --- the study degenerates, which is a limitation of the design
worth naming rather than a property of the methods.

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:prompts}

\paragraph{Proposer (system).}
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You audit the adjacency structure of a causal graph estimated from a small sample. The world is an
unknown linear-Gaussian DAG with no hidden confounders and full observability. You are given the
correlation matrix, the partial correlation of every pair given all other variables, and the
adjacency set the PC algorithm returned from the same sample. Read the statistics like this: for a
linear-Gaussian DAG, two variables are directly connected if and only if their partial correlation
given all others is non-zero. So a pair with a sizeable partial correlation that PC left
non-adjacent is a likely FALSE NEGATIVE, and a pair PC made adjacent whose partial correlation is
near zero is a likely FALSE POSITIVE. With a small sample PC's independence tests are underpowered,
so borderline values matter. List only the pairs you would genuinely bet on --- an empty list is a
valid and often correct answer. Do not restate the whole graph; report edits only. At most 4
removals and 4 additions. Call propose\_skeleton\_edits exactly once.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Proposer (user).} A JSON object with the variable list, the observational summary
(row count, means, standard deviations, correlation matrix, full-order partial correlation
matrix), PC's adjacency list, and the list of currently non-adjacent pairs.

\paragraph{Whole-graph proposer.} The \texttt{LLM-graphs} arm receives the same statistics and is
asked for twelve distinct acyclic candidate DAGs, with instructions to vary the orientation of
edges the data cannot orient and to vary borderline adjacencies. It is told explicitly that a set
containing the truth is more valuable than a set of near-identical safe guesses.

\paragraph{Sanitizer.} Every model-produced structure passes through one shared routine before it
touches the pipeline: indices are coerced and range-checked, self-loops and duplicates are
dropped, removals are kept only if the pair is currently adjacent and additions only if it is not,
and any edge that would create a cycle is discarded. Malformed tool calls are re-prompted at most
twice and then counted as a failure. The proposal audit in Section~\ref{sec:wrong} scores edits
\emph{after} sanitization, so it measures what the system actually acted on.

\section{Additional results}
\label{app:extra}

\paragraph{Two ablations that do nothing, and why.}
Adding PC's equivalence class to the edit-derived candidate set changes no episode in any run
($0/40$ differences). This is not a null result but an identity: every member of PC's equivalence
class is an acyclic orientation of PC's skeleton, and the guard already reserves half the
hypothesis budget for the best-scoring such orientations, so the union adds nothing that survives
pruning. Likewise, submitting posterior edge marginals instead of the MAP graph reproduces the MAP
graph exactly, because the posterior concentrates hard --- mean MAP weight $0.961$, mean final
entropy $0.14$ nats. We report both because they bound how much of the design is load-bearing:
neither is.

\section{Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}

\paragraph{The proposer is asked to do statistics.}
With anonymized variables the model cannot use domain knowledge, only the correlation and partial
correlation matrices --- and reading a partial correlation against its sampling noise is close to
the competency our companion study showed these models lack. The near-chance precision we report
should therefore be read as a lower bound on what a proposer could contribute, not as a general
claim about LLMs and causality.

\paragraph{Exactness does not scale.}
The filtering that makes wrong proposals cheap depends on an exact posterior over an enumerated
candidate set, which is affordable to about $d = 12$. Beyond that the candidate set must be
sampled, and a sampled filter can miss the truth even when a proposal contains it. Whether the
error-cost argument survives that substitution is untested here.

\paragraph{One model class, one front-end.}
All results assume linear-Gaussian mechanisms, causal sufficiency and hard interventions, with PC
as the front-end. The crossover in Section~\ref{sec:crossover} is a statement about \emph{how
wrong the front-end is}, and we vary that through sample size only; varying it through the
significance threshold or through a different estimator would be a stronger test.

\paragraph{Effect sizes are small for one of two models.}
The LLM's contribution over the no-LLM pipeline is significant for \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}
($+0.060$, $p{=}0.0075$) but not for \texttt{qwen3-30b} ($+0.027$, $p{=}0.20$) in the main regime,
while at $n_{\mathrm{obs}}{=}40$ the ordering reverses. The headline comparisons against the
classical and end-to-end baselines are large and robust; the LLM-versus-no-LLM comparison is not,
and we have reported it at that strength throughout.
